The Quarterly Journal of Economics

Why Do Union Jobs Pay More? New Evidence from Matched Employer-Employee Data

Pierre-Loup Beauregard, Thomas Lemieux, Derek Messacar, Raffaele Saggio

Published online ahead of issue · November 5, 2026 · accepted manuscript

Publication or listing record · Text read · NBER Working Paper 33740, May 2025

Research Question

Why do union jobs pay more? The paper separates a premium tied to the types of firms where unions are present from the additional pay attributed to union bargaining. It also asks whether unionization changes the returns to worker skills.

Research Method

The authors estimate worker and firm effects using an Abowd–Kramarz–Margolis two-way fixed-effects model. Worker movements help separate portable earnings differences from firm pay premiums. They relate the estimated firm premiums to value added per worker and extend the model to let the portable worker component vary by union status. Reweighting workers who change union status extends the model-based estimates to unionized workers who do not switch.

Research Data

The manuscript links Canadian employer–employee administrative records, union-dues information and firm balance sheets. The worker panel covers 2001–2019. Value added per worker measures firm productivity, and moves across employers or union statuses inform the decomposition.

Research Findings

Unionized firms pay roughly 15 log points more after controlling for worker effects. About 40% of that gap is linked to higher productivity at unionized firms. The residual implies about 9 log points of additional pay for unionized workers, rising to about 11 in the model with heterogeneous skill returns. The estimates vary by worker skill, industry and firm cohort. The decomposition depends on the model's assumptions about how productivity and firm wage premiums are related.

Contribution

The paper connects the union wage premium to both the allocation of unions across firms and differences in rent sharing. It shows how allowing for wage compression changes the estimated union effect.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Compare the model-based decomposition with close union elections or changes in legal recognition.
  • Measure whether union bargaining changes benefits as well as pay.
  • Link productivity and wage changes over time to test whether the relationship differs at newly organized firms.

The Review of Economic Studies

Optimal Policy Rules in HANK

Alisdair McKay, Christian K. Wolf

Published online ahead of issue · November 7, 2026

Publication or listing record · Text read · authors’ April 2026 manuscript

Research Question

Does household inequality change the optimal way to stabilize inflation and output? The paper asks how interest rates and fiscal transfers should be used when the policymaker also cares about consumption inequality.

Research Method

The authors characterize forecast-targeting rules in a heterogeneous-agent New Keynesian model with nominal rigidities. The instruments are a short nominal interest rate and lump-sum transfers. They distinguish changes in how policy affects the economy from changes in the policymaker's objective. A key condition for the aggregate result is that household heterogeneity enters the demand side without changing aggregate supply relationships.

Research Data

The analysis combines theory, model calibration and evidence used to discipline monetary transmission and distributional responses. The welfare and instrument comparisons are model exercises. They do not come from a randomized comparison of monetary and fiscal stabilization regimes.

Research Findings

With a conventional aggregate dual mandate, the optimal target criterion is the same as in the analogous representative-agent model under the paper's conditions. A distributional objective adds a term depending on how instruments affect consumption inequality. In the calibrated economy, rates have relatively even consumption effects across households, so the additional term changes the optimal rate rule little. Transfers are more progressive and can cushion unequal cyclical losses.

Contribution

The paper separates the question of whether heterogeneity changes policy targets from whether it changes how those targets can be achieved. It also explains why the instrument that best manages aggregate demand may differ from the instrument that most directly addresses distributional losses.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Introduce household heterogeneity on the supply side, including sector-specific labor wedges.
  • Discipline consumption responses with linked spending and balance-sheet data around rate shocks.
  • Compare the transfer rule with realistic implementation lags and targeting constraints.

The Roles of Borrower Private Information and Mortgage Relief Design in Foreclosure Prevention

Lauri Kytömaa

Forthcoming · listed November 9, 2026

Publication or listing record · Text read · journal-hosted accepted manuscript

Research Question

What prevents lenders and servicers from modifying delinquent mortgages when foreclosure is costly? Kytömaa distinguishes hidden borrower financial circumstances, the cost of processing relief and the amount of relief borrowers receive.

Research Method

The paper estimates a structural game in which a servicer commits to a modification policy, borrowers privately observe their financial health and delinquency can be strategic. Borrowers differ in whether they will resume payments without assistance or can continue paying after modification. Variation in modification and loan outcomes across servicers helps identify the model. The estimated model is then used to simulate alternative designs of the Home Affordable Modification Program.

Research Data

The data follow around 162,000 Fannie Mae-guaranteed California mortgages originated between 2004 and 2007 through the end of 2019. The records include servicer identity, loan modifications, foreclosure outcomes and the proceeds and costs of foreclosures.

Research Findings

The model estimates that program incentive payments reduced foreclosures by about 3%. Information frictions raised foreclosures by 22% compared with a full-information counterfactual. The assistance level under the program would have been insufficient to avert 84% of foreclosures. These figures compare distinct model counterfactuals. In particular, improving screening and making the modification more generous address different sources of failed relief.

Contribution

The framework puts lender incentives, strategic delinquency and relief sufficiency into one analysis. It can distinguish whether an intervention should lower servicing costs, improve information or change payment relief.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Test whether verified income shocks improve targeting without deterring applications.
  • Compare temporary payment relief with principal reduction under alternative house-price paths.
  • Estimate how foreclosure and modification rules in other states affect the results.

Taxing Homeowners Who Can’t or Won’t Borrow

Francis Wong

Forthcoming · listed November 9, 2026

Publication or listing record · Text read · journal-hosted September 2026 manuscript

Research Question

How do homeowners adjust when rising home values raise their property-tax bills? Wong asks whether home equity helps them finance the increase and where the welfare costs arise.

Research Method

The paper uses reassessment-driven differences in tax bills to study changes in spending, debt, distress and migration. The author compares responses by liquid resources, credit access and home equity, checks responses to tax decreases, and uses a homeowner survey to investigate reluctance to borrow. A simple welfare framework translates the estimated behavioral responses into policy comparisons.

Research Data

The main financial-behavior sample has 4.9 million loan-year tax increases for US properties assessed from 2010 to 2019. It links credit bureau and mortgage-servicing data and focuses on owners who pay taxes through mortgage escrow, which identifies the month payments rise. Separate records capture moves, and a survey measures homeowners' borrowing preferences.

Research Findings

Homeowners who stay cut consumption and experience more distress, with little evidence of borrowing against higher home equity. Spending responses appear across liquidity groups, while distress is concentrated among liquidity-constrained owners. Survey respondents frequently express debt aversion, including reluctance to take up tax deferrals. Tax increases also induce some migration, partly among illiquid homeowners. The welfare exercise assigns a large role to distress costs.

Contribution

The paper distinguishes home equity from cash available to pay a tax bill and separates inability to borrow from unwillingness to borrow. This helps explain why a tax deferral can be less effective than its interest rate alone would suggest.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Randomize the framing and application burden of tax deferrals.
  • Compare payment installments with income-based relief using linked credit records.
  • Test whether responses differ in places with more elastic housing supply.

November 9 release · 21 working papers. The identifiers below are the set reported by NBER and the dated archive.

Target What? The Foundations of Poverty Targeting

Rema Hanna, Emily Breza, Benjamin A. Olken, Abhijit Banerjee, Arun G. Chandrasekhar, Gideon Moore

NBER Working Paper 35984 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Should poverty programs target people with the lowest consumption, those who feel worst off or those whom the community believes would gain most from a transfer? The answer depends on which welfare concept the program is meant to serve.

Research Method

The authors elicit rankings of welfare levels and expected gains from a modest cash transfer, compare these rankings with expenditure and self-assessed welfare, and analyze a randomized comparison of targeting methods. The community rankings are measures of villagers' judgments, rather than direct observations of each household's utility gain.

Research Data

The study covers 600 Indonesian villages and surveys more than 5,500 villagers. It combines local rankings of other households, expenditure and subjective welfare measures, and evidence from alternative targeting procedures.

Research Findings

Targeting consumption misses 77% of households the community assigns the highest marginal social valuation. Targeting self-assessed welfare misses 57%, and targeting the households the community ranks worst off in levels misses 25%. Proxy means tests perform best when the objective is low consumption. An application ordeal aligns more closely with self-assessed welfare, while community targeting is closest to the community's view of who would benefit most.

Contribution

The paper makes the normative choice behind targeting explicit. It shows why a targeting procedure can perform well by one criterion and poorly by another even when each is implemented carefully.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Compare rankings with later transfer effects on nutrition and schooling.
  • Test whether anonymous elicitation reduces favoritism or strategic reporting.
  • Repeat the exercise after shocks to see how stable the rankings and their predictors are.

Unconventional Monetary Policy and Deposit Retention

Philip Strahan, Tuo Yang

NBER Working Paper 35985 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Did the zero lower bound and quantitative easing alter which depositors funded banks, and what happened to funding costs once rates rose?

Research Method

The authors exploit differences in bank exposure to both policies and estimate how deposit flows respond to relative deposit rates. Their QE exposure measure uses granular Federal Reserve bond-purchase transactions and geographic market overlap with primary dealers through which sales are initially intermediated. This helps distinguish exposure to asset purchases from the simultaneous compression of short rates.

Research Data

The study combines US bank and local-market deposit measures, deposit rates and Federal Reserve bond transactions. It examines post-crisis unconventional policy and subsequent rate increases, including lift-offs in late 2015 and 2022. Insured and uninsured deposits provide a useful contrast.

Research Findings

Very low rates compressed the returns available from shopping around, drawing rate-sensitive funds into banks. QE purchases from nonbanks also brought in large deposits, with strong effects for uninsured deposits. After rates rose, both flow sensitivity and the deposit beta increased more at exposed banks. The paper interprets that pattern as evidence that unconventional policy changed the customer base, increasing the cost and risk of retention.

Contribution

The analysis links monetary policy today to the elasticity of bank funding during a later tightening. It separates the portfolio flows induced by QE from the lower opportunity cost of holding deposits during the zero-rate period.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Trace incoming balances to individual households or firms to test the composition channel directly.
  • Examine how deposit insurance changes the effect.
  • Compare asset purchases routed through different intermediaries and bank-market structures.

Debiasing Law Enforcement Officers: Evidence from an Expressive Arts Intervention in India

Sofia Amaral, Girija Borker, Nishith Prakash, María Micaela Sviatschi

NBER Working Paper 35986 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Can training change the way incumbent police officers handle gender-based violence, and do changes in stated attitudes translate into better treatment of survivors?

Research Method

The authors partnered with Bihar Police to randomize a three-day expressive-arts curriculum grounded in Theatre of the Oppressed across police stations. They measure attitudes, empathy and legal knowledge and later use standardized victim audits and official records to track behavior. Officer transfers provide additional, suggestive evidence on persistence and peer spillovers.

Research Data

The trial covers 417 police stations and roughly 2,500 active-duty senior field officers. Data include surveys about seven months after baseline, standardized victim audits about a year later, administrative records on case registration and reports, and surveys of junior women officers.

Research Findings

Treated officers showed greater empathy and legal knowledge. In audits, officers at treated stations were about 15 percentage points less likely to blame survivors and more likely to provide referrals. Case-registration delays fell 41%. Later reporting increased, a pattern consistent with improved access rather than necessarily an increase in underlying violence. Junior women officers working with treated seniors reported less harassment and anxiety.

Contribution

The paper measures how changes in attitudes can alter decisions by officials who exercise substantial discretion. The audits and records help distinguish a response to survey questions from a change in service delivery.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Randomize booster sessions to measure how long the training effect lasts.
  • Compare expressive arts with legal training of equal duration.
  • Link registrations to case resolution and survivor outcomes to trace downstream effects.

TaskNet: Building Occupational Task Inventories from Worker Interviews at Scale

Jennifer Wang, Andreas Haupt, Isaac Robinson, Phil Lewis, Erik Brynjolfsson, Diyi Yang

NBER Working Paper 35987 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Can researchers build an occupational task inventory directly from workers' descriptions, and would the resulting inventory change estimates of AI exposure?

Research Method

TaskNet uses semi-structured interviews with adaptive follow-up questions, worker review of individual task lists and a procedure for aggregating overlapping descriptions. The authors validate the resulting statements with O*NET analysts, other workers, held-out interviews and job postings. The validation checks coverage and usefulness, rather than assuming any one inventory is ground truth.

Research Data

The pilot covers six occupations. Interviews with 132 workers yield 2,726 task reports and 202 aggregate task statements. Another 318 workers provide 7,923 ratings. Benchmarks include O*NET 31.0 and employer job postings.

Research Findings

ONET analysts deemed all 202 statements valid for emerging-task review. TaskNet covers about 75% of the 118 existing statements, adds 79 tasks without an ONET counterpart and refines another 58. It captures more activities in held-out interviews and changes AI exposure scores in several occupations. Newly identified coordination tasks illustrate why exposure can reflect complementarity or substitution.

Contribution

The study offers a replicable way to update task descriptions as work changes. It shows how measurement at the task level can alter the distribution of estimated AI exposure, even when occupation labels stay fixed.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Repeat interviews across firms and over time to detect genuine task change.
  • Compare results from AI-led and human-led interviews using the same sampling frame.
  • Link newly identified tasks to actual AI use and changes in worker responsibilities.

The Hidden Sharpe Ratio

Mikhail Chernov, Bryan T. Kelly, Semyon Malamud, Johannes Schwab

NBER Working Paper 35988 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

How high is the maximum Sharpe ratio in a large asset universe? Standard estimates can miss profitable directions in return space when the number of assets is large relative to the available history.

Research Method

The authors estimate the squared maximum directly with higher-order debiased statistics, recovering contributions from small covariance eigenvalues that ridge regularization suppresses. They derive a lower bound and construct an upper bound under an additional condition on the risk–return relationship. They also characterize the shortfall of a ridge-regularized Markowitz portfolio.

Research Data

The application uses US equity returns and stock characteristics, comparing broad and non-microcap universes, linear and nonlinear portfolio weights and specifications that add corporate bonds. The universe and the mapping from characteristics to weights define which payoff directions the estimate can capture.

Research Findings

The estimated annualized maximum is about 7.4 for the broader equity universe and 5.0 without microcaps. Adding corporate bonds raises the estimate by a statistically insignificant amount. Nonlinear weights outperform linear ones in the analysis. Proposition 4 derives a nonvanishing shortfall for ridge portfolios, and the paper cautions that the closeness of its bounds can partly reflect how the threshold is chosen.

Contribution

The paper separates estimation of an efficient frontier from construction of a portfolio that would attain it. Its estimates characterize a statistical opportunity set. Trading costs, leverage and implementation can limit what an investor realizes.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Estimate the frontier after realistic trading and financing costs.
  • Test the sensitivity of the bounds to regime changes and clustered returns.
  • Evaluate whether portfolio rules designed around the small-eigenvalue directions remain stable out of sample.

Clearing the Fog: Price Transparency, Market Churn, and Consumer Welfare in Short-Term Rentals

Ginger Zhe Jin, Liad Wagman, Jingyi Xing

NBER Working Paper 35989 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

What happens to prices, host participation and guest welfare when short-term rentals must display mandatory fees upfront? The analysis considers changes in which listings remain available as well as how guests respond to any given listing.

Research Method

The authors compare changes around California's July 1, 2024 all-in pricing law using matched difference-in-differences. Coarsened exact matching selects comparable listings. A discrete-choice model decomposes changes in realized consumer surplus into greater fee salience, pricing and changes in listings. The comparison relies on credible counterfactual trends in the matched markets.

Research Data

The data follow Airbnb listings for roughly a year before and after the law. San Diego and San Francisco are compared with Seattle and Chicago. Prices, fees, quality measures and entry and exit are observed across ZIP codes.

Research Findings

Guests become more price sensitive, yet all-in daily prices rise about 4.8% relative to comparison cities. Listings that relied more on cleaning fees are more likely to exit, higher-quality listings are more likely to stay, and entrants offer lower fees and lower quality than incumbents. Model-based welfare gains are positive on average and geographically uneven, with changes in available listings an important part of the result.

Contribution

The study shows why a transparency mandate can affect market composition enough to change its welfare consequences. Consumer surplus can rise even when the average transaction option becomes more expensive.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Compare the response with platforms that already displayed all-in prices.
  • Test whether multi-platform hosts shift supply across platforms.
  • Estimate how the effects vary by trip duration and the prevalence of cleaning fees.

Financial Advisors and Retirees' Risk-taking

Allison T. Cole, Maarten Meeuwis, Alessandro Previtero, Noah Stoffman

NBER Working Paper 35990 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Why do advised retirees hold more stocks than common age-based rules imply? The study asks whether the difference reflects adviser influence and whether it makes sense given the retirees' other resources.

Research Method

The authors compare allocations and use changes of adviser to study gradual movement toward the incoming adviser's style. Relationship tenure and client characteristics help assess the role of trust. A life-cycle model with safe pension income evaluates allocations and a counterfactual in which advisers correct pessimistic beliefs about stock returns.

Research Data

Administrative portfolio records cover more than 36,000 advised Canadian retirees. The paper uses holdings, transactions, adviser changes and available measures of financial knowledge and risk tolerance. Comparisons include non-advised portfolios, target-date funds and common rules of thumb.

Research Findings

Advised retirees keep about two-thirds of their portfolios in equities, roughly 30–40 percentage points above the comparison benchmarks. Trades and responses to adviser changes point to active adjustment. Advisers have more influence among clients with lower knowledge or risk tolerance. Safe pension income makes high financial-portfolio equity shares consistent with moderate risk aversion in the model. The estimated welfare gain from taking more risk is of similar magnitude to the cost of advice under the belief-correction counterfactual.

Contribution

The paper uses the retiree's full resources, including pensions, to interpret portfolio risk. It also distinguishes adviser influence from simply inheriting an allocation at retirement.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Compare responses where pension income is less secure.
  • Use adviser retirements or firm closures to strengthen the switch design.
  • Separate the value of belief correction from the value of help with taxes and withdrawals.

Doing Better at Doing Good: Lessons from a Bayesian Meta-Analysis After 20 Years of Poverty Graduation Programs

Samantha Carter, Edward Jee, Dean Karlan, Patrick Premand

NBER Working Paper 35991 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

How effective are multifaceted poverty graduation programs, and which program designs deliver more improvement per dollar spent?

Research Method

The paper pools estimated impacts in a Bayesian hierarchical meta-analysis. Qualifying programs contain at least three of five components, including training, coaching, consumption support, savings facilitation and a large one-time transfer. The authors compare study cohorts and simulate benefit–cost ratios under assumptions about persistence. Comparisons across program designs are less directly identified than treatment effects within randomized evaluations.

Research Data

The corpus contains 49 studies and 81 treatment variants across multiple settings. The analysis tracks economic and psychosocial outcomes, program costs, component bundles and implementation dates.

Research Findings

Estimated average monthly consumption and income rise by about 38 and 28 purchasing-power-adjusted US dollars, or 11% and 15%, respectively. The paper also finds improvements in savings, food security and some psychosocial outcomes. Programs with transfers of at least 400 PPP dollars have larger impacts without a clear cost-effectiveness advantage. Under the preferred persistence scenario, benefit–cost ratios rise from 2.89 through 2015 to 5.39 for programs starting in 2016 or later. The observable changes in components and costs do not explain that difference.

Contribution

The analysis makes program costs and heterogeneity central to interpreting a growing intervention literature. It separates average impact from the return to spending more on the package.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Randomize individual components within a common implementation model.
  • Compare scaling costs and performance in nonprofit and government delivery.
  • Follow cohorts longer to test the persistence assumptions behind the benefit–cost estimates.

Policy Deterrence: Strategic Investment in U.S. Broadband

Karam Kang, Mo Xiao

NBER Working Paper 35992 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Can incumbent broadband firms use investment to reduce the political demand for policies that would help smaller providers enter or expand?

Research Method

The authors examine whether large providers expand more in electorally competitive counties and whether capacity in those counties predicts subsequent state policies. They develop a model in which an incumbent's investment lowers a policymaker's perceived gain from intervention. The observed relationships are interpreted in light of that mechanism, rather than treated as direct estimates of strategic intent.

Research Data

The study links US county broadband deployment from 2010 to 2019 with electoral competition and state policies. It distinguishes large and small providers and examines rights-of-way accommodations that ease infrastructure deployment.

Research Findings

Large providers expand more in politically competitive counties. Greater large-provider capacity in swing counties is associated with fewer subsequent pro-entry policies, especially rights-of-way accommodations. In the model, deterring such policy raises the large firm's share and can reduce total deployment when smaller firms invest less. The paper does not estimate the net consumer-welfare effect.

Contribution

The study identifies business investment itself as a possible instrument of political influence. It clarifies how some capacity expansion can accompany a reduction in future competitive entry.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Use close changes in electoral competitiveness to test where investment shifts.
  • Compare outcomes when states precommit to rights-of-way rules.
  • Link investment and entry to subsequent prices, service quality and coverage.

Peer Influence in College Applications

Ana M. Gazmuri, Elena Prager

NBER Working Paper 35993 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Does exposure to peers who apply to particular college programs change where students apply? The paper asks whether peer information can help explain application gaps among otherwise comparable students.

Research Method

The authors use exposure through neighbors' schoolmates at different high schools to reduce contamination by common school shocks. They estimate a portfolio-choice model in a setting with observable and conditionally independent admission probabilities, which makes the application problem tractable. The comparison separates exposure to a peer's application from any added effect of the peer being admitted.

Research Data

Administrative data cover Ontario secondary students applying to universities from 2011 to 2016. The records link applications and admissions to detailed school and classroom information and geographically defined peer exposure.

Research Findings

Exposure to peers who applied to a program increases application rates. Admission of the peer adds little beyond the application itself, consistent with information about the option being important. Model counterfactuals suggest that changing peer exposure can close an urban–rural gap in selective applications and narrow gender gaps in selective or STEM programs. These are simulated changes, not estimates of relocating students.

Contribution

The paper explains how information diffuses into choices among programs, extending work that focuses on whether a student applies to college at all. Its setting also offers a tractable approach to estimating choices over multiple applications.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Randomize program information within networks to test spillovers directly.
  • Compare information about program existence with information about fit and returns.
  • Track students into graduation and earnings to see whether the changed application mix improves outcomes.

The Impact of Climate Change on Fisheries

Fabio Cevenini, Christopher Costello, Carlo Fezzi

NBER Working Paper 35994 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

How does exposure to ocean temperatures outside a species' thermal range affect commercial fishing revenue, and where are climate-related losses likely to concentrate?

Research Method

The authors build Marine Degree Days around species-specific thermal tolerances and estimate the effect of deviations from those ranges. A high-dimensional fixed-effects strategy uses differences across species exposed to the same local conditions and controls for ecological and spatial confounders. The projections then apply the estimated relationship to future temperature scenarios.

Research Data

The study uses roughly 2.5 million spatially explicit observations of fishing revenue for European vessels from 2013 through 2022, combined with ocean temperatures and species biology. It distinguishes species with different vertical mobility and other ecological characteristics.

Research Findings

Thermal deviations reduce revenue, with heat exposure producing larger losses. Effects vary across species and locations. The warmest modeled scenario implies annual losses of around €560 million by 2100, with more severe losses in lower-income equatorial areas and gains in some cooler places. The projections depend on the climate scenario and the assumed response of fleets and species.

Contribution

The paper connects a biological exposure measure to an economically observed outcome. It provides a way to compare thermal sensitivity across species without treating the same ocean temperature as equally harmful to every catch.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Model shifts in fleet location, gear and species composition jointly with warming.
  • Track the distribution of losses among ports and fishing households.
  • Compare catch or revenue estimates under alternative quota systems and adaptation assumptions.

When Food Stamps Became Free: Impacts and Implications of Removing the Purchase Requirement

Amy Finkelstein, Matthew J. Notowidigdo, Charlie Rafkin

NBER Working Paper 35996 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Did making food stamps free increase enrollment, improve infant health and make the program more redistributive? The paper distinguishes the value of transferring resources from the value of investing in children's health.

Research Method

The authors exploit county differences in exposure to the 1979 removal of the purchase requirement. Counties with more eligible nonparticipants before the reform had more room for enrollment to rise. They use this variation to estimate enrollment effects and instrument food-stamp participation in the infant-health analysis. Take-up behavior also informs bounds on how much recipients value the benefit.

Research Data

Sources include county food-stamp enrollment, population and poverty counts, US birth records, the Panel Study of Income Dynamics and a food consumption survey. The authors compare the characteristics of new enrollees with those of existing participants.

Research Findings

Enrollment rises 1.85 percentage points, about 25%, and marginal enrollees are substantially poorer. The authors find no detectable infant-health improvement. Their combined estimates rule out an increase in birth weight greater than about 2%. Estimated private valuation of free benefits is about 59 cents per dollar, with bounds of 42–77 cents. The welfare calculation still favors the program under standard redistributive weights because benefits reach very low-income households.

Contribution

The reform separates the distributional effect of removing a participation cost from evidence about health. Free benefits improve targeting by social value in the authors' framework even though the recipients' private valuation per dollar is lower than for those previously selected into participation.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Compare in-kind benefits and cash under equal take-up costs.
  • Link historical enrollment to outcomes after childhood.
  • Test which benefit features explain low measured private valuation by comparing reforms that alter choice, application costs or redemption rules.

Beyond Overdose Prevention: Naloxone Coprescription Laws and the Path to Treatment

Shin-Yi Chou, Jiarun Li, Bingjin Xue

NBER Working Paper 35997 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Do naloxone coprescription laws change prescribing and engagement with opioid-use-disorder treatment beyond increasing access to an overdose-reversal drug?

Research Method

The authors compare states before and after adoption with states without the same law using difference-in-differences. They distinguish laws that require prescribing from those that require offering naloxone. Outcomes track both prescribing behavior and entry into treatment, with a separate analysis of referral pathways.

Research Data

Data span 2013–2023 and include Medicare Part D prescribing records and admissions in the Treatment Episode Data Set. State law characteristics identify timing and whether the legal requirement is a prescription or an offer.

Research Findings

Coprescription laws increase measured naloxone prescribing by 275%, with larger effects under prescription requirements. The analysis finds no evidence of increased opioid or benzodiazepine prescribing. Medications for opioid use disorder and treatment admissions rise, with gains concentrated in settings consistent with deliberate treatment seeking. The estimates concern prescribing and admissions in the observed data.

Contribution

The study examines how a prescribing mandate can affect contacts with treatment rather than treating naloxone access as the only outcome. It also tests for a prescribing response that could have offset gains.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Link prescriptions to pharmacy fills and longer-run treatment retention.
  • Compare mandates where access to treatment providers differs.
  • Use clinic-level workflow changes to test whether the treatment response arises from conversations, referrals or changes in prescriber behavior.

Sorting by Participation: A Field Experiment in a Stem-cell Registry

Michael Haylock, Patrick Kampkötter, Mario Macis, Susanne Seitz, Robert Slonim, Daniel Wiesen

NBER Working Paper 35998 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Can an engagement invitation help a stem-cell registry identify people who will be available when called for confirmatory typing? The question is whether the invitation changes reliability or helps the registry observe it.

Research Method

The authors randomize the framing of routine outreach. Experimental letters make the registry's request more salient, and two add appeals to being quickly available or highly committed. Intention-to-treat comparisons estimate how framing affects participation and availability. Differences between opt-in and non-opt-in donors measure sorting, since choosing to participate is voluntary.

Research Data

The experiment is conducted with DKMS, using outreach responses and administrative outcomes when registered donors are contacted for confirmatory typing. The paper also models how using participation as a signal could affect survival outcomes.

Research Findings

More salient or identity-framed letters reduce participation relative to the usual invitation. The treatments have no detectable average effect on availability. Among donors later called, 12% of participants are unavailable versus 30% of nonparticipants. Simulations suggest up to 7.5% more one-year survivors if a registry uses the signal to prioritize contacts in the studied setting. That figure is a projected relative gain.

Contribution

The paper shows how a voluntary action can reveal reliability even when it does not improve reliability on average. This distinction matters for designing outreach and deciding how to use the resulting information.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Randomize search prioritization to validate the simulated gains.
  • Assess whether opt-in flags lose predictive value over time.
  • Test whether using the flag affects equitable access to matches for patients with uncommon tissue types.

(In)Credible Development RCTs: False Discoveries, Low Power, and a Solution

Qingyuan Chai, Kevin Lang, Liang Zhong

NBER Working Paper 35999 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

How credible are published statistically significant results from development RCTs, and how should a funder allocate a fixed budget for replication?

Research Method

The authors compare the distribution of published test statistics with benchmarks for power and possible specification search. An empirical Bayes mixture model adjusts for selective publication and estimates the share of false discoveries. A separate optimization problem chooses which results to replicate and the replication sample size.

Research Data

The study uses 745 tests of principal hypotheses from 190 articles in leading economics journals. The estimated false-discovery rates apply to this corpus and depend on the model for underlying effects and selection into publication.

Research Findings

The estimated false-discovery share among published rejections is about 30%, and average directional power is about 40%. For results near conventional significance thresholds, the estimated probability of a false discovery reaches 66%. In the calibrated policy exercise, replication targets an intermediate range of original test statistics, with replication sample size largest inside that range. Illustrative successful replications reduce aggregate false discoveries to 1.8–7.8% depending on the evidence standard.

Contribution

The paper makes replication a decision about where an additional experiment can change the quality of evidence most. It distinguishes the value of rechecking intermediate results from simply requiring bigger samples or more stringent significance in every original study.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Validate the publication-selection model against preregistered and unpublished trials.
  • Allow replications to vary in settings and treatment implementation.
  • Include a second replication when the first leaves substantial uncertainty.

When More Prenatal Care Looks Like Less: Selection, Information and Maternal and Infant Health

Chien-Tzu Cheng, Shin-Yi Chou, Hsien-Ming Lien

NBER Working Paper 36000 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

How much does prenatal care improve maternal and infant health, and why can a simple comparison between patients with more and fewer visits give a misleading answer?

Research Method

The authors instrument adequate care with the timing of gestational exposure to Taiwan's unanticipated 2003 SARS disruption. They check whether direct effects of the epidemic could explain the outcome patterns and compare instrumental-variable estimates with ordinary least squares. The causal interpretation requires the disruption to affect these outcomes through changes in care after accounting for other channels.

Research Data

The study links Taiwan administrative records from 2001–2003, including National Health Insurance claims and delivery and birth records. Gestational exposure varies across pregnancies and reduced prenatal visits by more than 20% in the relevant period.

Research Findings

Adequate care reduces preventable maternal delivery complications by 3.4 percentage points, about 26%. There is no statistically detectable effect on the infant outcomes examined. Ordinary least squares gives a maternal estimate with the opposite sign and overstates the infant benefit relative to the instrumental-variable results. Mechanism tests are consistent with repeated visits generating information that informs delivery decisions.

Contribution

The paper distinguishes selection into care from the benefits of care. It also directs attention to the information generated across multiple visits rather than treating visits as independent, identical services.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Compare disrupted visits by stage of pregnancy.
  • Measure whether electronic records transmit the relevant risk information across providers.
  • Replicate the design in settings with different visit schedules and referral systems.

Vigilance, Risk Vulnerability, and the Precautionary Augment

Miles S. Kimball, Vincent A. Mastantuno

NBER Working Paper 36001 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

How should economists compare the strength of precautionary saving across people exposed to background risk? The authors want a measure that also maps into equilibrium asset prices.

Research Method

The precautionary augment is the ratio of expected marginal utility under risk to marginal utility at the certainty benchmark, holding saving fixed. The paper defines greater vigilance as a larger augment for every unfair risk, meaning a risk with nonpositive expected payoff in the comparison. A geometric implementation of the Diffidence Theorem gives conditions for ordering utility functions.

Research Data

This is a theoretical paper. The examples concern utility functions, a Lucas-tree economy and derivative-rich asset markets in which traded securities still leave some idiosyncratic risk uninsured. The paper does not estimate preferences from household data.

Research Findings

The paper derives necessary and sufficient conditions involving pairs of wealth outcomes and compares simpler sufficient conditions based on risk aversion and prudence. Decreasing vigilance with wealth corresponds to risk vulnerability, allowing classic results about background risk to follow from the new comparison. In the asset-pricing applications, the augment summarizes how uninsurable risk shifts marginal valuations.

Contribution

The measure complements the precautionary premium. It focuses on the proportional change in marginal utility at a given saving level, which makes the connection to pricing kernels and risk-free rates direct.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Estimate bounds on vigilance from joint choices over saving and insurance.
  • Extend the comparison to preferences with habits or recursive utility.
  • Study equilibrium prices when the extent of derivative coverage depends on wealth or borrowing limits.

Knowledge Without Taxation? Experimental Evidence from a Tax Education Program

Anders Jensen, Nikhil Kumar, Konstantin Poensgen

NBER Working Paper 36002 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Does teaching taxpayers and frontline officials how property taxation works improve compliance where baseline knowledge and enforcement capacity are low?

Research Method

The authors run a multi-arm randomized field experiment. Arms vary education for property owners, information given to officials about treated locations and training for officials. The design measures knowledge spillovers among untreated neighbors and separates learning from changes in payment, enforcement beliefs and interactions with officials.

Research Data

The study is set in an urban Ghanaian municipality. Surveys measure tax knowledge, perceived enforcement and relationships with officials. Administrative records capture tax outcomes. Variation in social exposure helps identify whether practical information travels beyond directly treated households.

Research Findings

Education increases practical and declarative knowledge, with practical knowledge also spreading to untreated neighbors. The study detects no improvement in payment. Interactions become more engaged without clear gains in cooperation. Taxpayers learn about legal ways to reduce liabilities and revise down their initially high beliefs about enforcement, though the study does not find greater use of those legal options.

Contribution

The paper shows how a policy can successfully transmit information without producing the compliance response that motivated it. Knowledge can change both the cost of complying and what taxpayers expect the state can enforce.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Randomize education together with credible improvements in enforcement.
  • Separate messages about rights, procedures and penalties.
  • Follow the effects over multiple tax cycles to test whether engagement eventually changes cooperation or accountability.

AI as Shared Business Infrastructure: Firm Growth and the Distribution of Gains

Panle Jia Barwick, Lu Fang, Jinhan Liu, Huilan Xu, Zhe Yuan

NBER Working Paper 36003 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Do task-level gains from generative AI translate into firm growth? The paper examines whether a shared platform lowers the barriers to adoption enough for smaller firms to benefit most.

Research Method

The authors compare adopters with matched firms before and after adoption using a staggered cohort design. A complementary instrument combines preexisting reliance on tasks AI can perform, provincial digitization and diffusion in other product categories. The authors use operational and market outcomes to examine whether firms grow by reducing routine costs, improving information or taking sales from rivals.

Research Data

The study uses linked AI usage logs, sales and operating records from a large Chinese e-commerce platform that embedded AI in seller software in 2024. The matched analysis focuses on firms with annual sales below RMB 5 million and tracks monthly outcomes through July 2025.

Research Findings

Monthly sales rise 51.6% and orders 47.0% in the matched estimates, with little change in average order price. Gains are proportionally larger among the smallest sellers. Adopters reach more customers, introduce products faster and improve several measures of service quality while using less staff time per order. Category-level patterns are consistent with market expansion, though the setting and time horizon matter for interpreting aggregate effects.

Contribution

The paper studies AI as an input supplied centrally through an existing operating platform. This distinguishes the scale needed to develop AI from the scale needed by the firms using it.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Follow firms long enough to measure employment and wage effects.
  • Compare platform-supplied AI with independently purchased tools.
  • Test whether lower information frictions also change market concentration as competing platforms deploy similar systems.

The Main Monetary Policy Shock

Christoph Boehm, Husang Kim, T. Niklas Kroner

NBER Working Paper 36004 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

Which combination of observed monetary policy surprises accounts for the most variation in output and prices, and does it resemble the conventional interest-rate shock?

Research Method

The authors fit a VAR and choose a linear combination of two yield-curve surprises and a non-yield surprise to maximize the cumulative contribution to industrial production and headline CPI variance. The baseline weights the two outcomes equally over four years. A causal interpretation relies on the monetary surprises being insulated from contemporaneous nonmonetary shocks.

Research Data

The application uses monthly US data from January 1996 to July 2025 and high-frequency measures of monetary news. The study distinguishes two yield-curve factors from a factor constructed to capture news outside those yield movements.

Research Findings

The main combination explains meaningful business-cycle variation, with the non-yield component important for the result. A contractionary shock persistently lowers headline prices and appreciates the dollar, while temporarily tightening financial conditions and depressing industrial production. Interest rates move relatively little. These are responses to the combination selected for the chosen outcomes and horizon.

Contribution

The method searches a space of high-frequency surprises for the variation that matters most for aggregate outcomes. It can reveal a monetary transmission channel that a narrower interest-rate measure would miss.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Evaluate the selected combination in a held-out period.
  • Vary the target variables and horizons to test stability.
  • Use announcement text and international market responses to investigate what information the non-yield factor captures.

Policy Evaluation Beyond Significance Testing: Evidence from Chicago's ShotSpotter Contract

Terence Chau, Philip J. Cook, Javier Lopez, Jens Ludwig, Alejandro Roemer, Adam K. Soliman

NBER Working Paper 36005 · November 9 batch

Publication or listing record · Text read · released NBER manuscript

Research Question

How should policymakers decide whether to continue a program when its estimated benefits could exceed costs but the effect is imprecisely measured? The application asks whether Chicago's acoustic gunfire detection system improved survival conditional on being shot.

Research Method

The authors use a geographic regression discontinuity comparing shootings near boundaries of Chicago police districts with and without ShotSpotter alerts. They translate the estimated fatality effect into benefits and use Bayesian decision analysis to compare those benefits with contract costs. The design relies on comparability near boundaries and the welfare conclusion depends on the assumed prior and value of statistical life.

Research Data

The analysis uses Chicago shooting records, shooting locations and district coverage. The main outcome is death among shooting victims. The decision exercise also uses contract costs and alternative values assigned to mortality reductions.

Research Findings

The point estimate is a 2.5 percentage point reduction in the fatality rate, equivalent to roughly 49 fewer deaths per year if the estimate is correct. The estimate is statistically imprecise. Across the reported assumptions, the posterior probability that benefits exceed costs ranges from 70% to 99.6%. The high end combines a no-harm prior with a high value of statistical life.

Contribution

The paper shows why the policy decision depends on the distribution of effects around a break-even threshold. The application makes the role of assumptions about uncertainty and the value of outcomes visible.

Potential Extensions

Proposed research directions.

  • Pool comparable deployments to improve precision.
  • Compare estimates using preexisting emergency-response capacity on each side of a boundary.
  • Incorporate additional costs and benefits such as false dispatches and changes in police activity.
